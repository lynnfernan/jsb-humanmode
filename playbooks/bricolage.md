# Bricolage

**Command:** Tap the Rest of You  
**Subtitle:** Lead with what you have — memory, tools, and people already in the room  
**Promise:** Solve unprecedented problems with what’s already here, not the perfect plan you don’t have yet.  
**Author:** Jeffrey Sanchez-Burks · *Human Mode* (HarperCollins, 2027), Part II  
**Companion to:** The Human Mode, Always™ Field Kit · free digital practice  
**Ethos:** Human Mode, Always.

## The meeting that waited for perfect resources

You have been in the room.

The problem is real. The timeline is short. Someone says, not unkindly, that you cannot move until headcount lands, until the vendor is chosen, until the data is complete, until the budget cycle turns.

So the room waits.

Not because there is nothing to work with. Because the room has been trained to treat *missing ideal conditions* as a stop sign. The people at the table have already solved stranger problems in other lives, other jobs, other countries, other constraints, and none of that is invited into the conversation.

That invitation is the skill.

**Bricolage** is adaptive resourcefulness: creating solutions from whatever is at hand rather than waiting for ideal conditions. In leadership, it is the practice of using available tools, people, memory, and knowledge when the playbook does not exist yet.

You do not become a Bricoleur by romanticizing chaos. You become one by **foraging your full human portfolio** under pressure, and by turning what you find into a rough, usable next move.

Everyone tells you to think outside the box. Work mode locked the boxes worth opening: a summer job you hated at sixteen, your grandmother’s garden, a film you’d swear you forgot.

---

## What a Bricoleur is (and isn’t)

| A Bricoleur is… | A Bricoleur is not… |
|-----------------|---------------------|
| A skillful **forager** of lived experience | A scrounger who never asks for real resources when needed |
| Someone who **recombines** tools and people at hand | Someone who glorifies underfunding as virtue |
| Comfortable shipping a **rough artifact** | Someone who performs scrappiness for show |
| Curious about *who have I known who solved something like this?* | Someone who only trusts best practice from elsewhere |

**Engineer vs. Bricoleur (working distinction):**  
The engineer waits for the right materials and the clean plan. The Bricoleur starts with the materials already on the table, including the human ones, and builds something that can be tested *today*.

Most hard problems need both. The failure mode in modern leadership is almost always the same: we over-index on waiting for ideal, and under-index on foraging what we already have.

## The three materials

Before you escalate the gap list, inventory these three.

### 1. Lived memory
People you have known. Roles you have held. Failures that taught you a shape. The grandmother who negotiated scarcity. The former boss who held a room. The child who asked the obvious question adults stopped asking.

### 2. Tools at hand
Software already licensed. Half-built process. Slack threads. A junior with data. A partner who owes you a favor. A template someone abandoned that still has one good page.

### 3. Constraints as design input
Time, money, politics, and “we can’t do that here” are not only obstacles. They are design parameters. A constraint forces combination. Combination is the Bricoleur’s craft.

## Exercise A — Overlooked resource deck

**When:** The official toolkit has already been listed and still fails.

**How:**
1. Name the stuck problem in one sentence.  
2. Draw one card from the locked boxes (person, tool, constraint, or lived scene).  
3. Ask: *How does this open a next move on my problem?*  
4. Write one usable step for this week.

**Do this week:** One draw before you escalate the gap list.

## Practice 1 — The 60-Second Memory Forage

*Digital twin of the Bricolage Lab (Human Mode Experience).*

**When:** You are stuck, or the room is waiting for conditions that will not arrive this week.

**How:**
1. Name the challenge in one sentence (out loud or on paper).  
2. Set a **60-second** timer.  
3. Close your eyes. Summon a specific person — grandmother, former boss, mentor, child, colleague from another industry.  
4. Ask: *How would they solve this with what they had?*  
5. Write the first usable move, not the perfect plan.

**Why it works:** It pulls non-work (and prior-work) intelligence into a problem that has been framed as “no resources.” Most of what you need is not missing. It is uninvited.

**Do this week:** Run the forage once before you send a “blocked on budget” note.

**Anti-pattern:** Treating the forage as nostalgia. The output is a **next move**, not a story.

---

## Practice 2 — Inventory the room before the gap list

**When:** The first slide of the meeting is “what we need.”

**How:** Flip the order for ten minutes.
1. What people are already in this room (or one Slack hop away)?  
2. What tools are already paid for?  
3. What prior attempts left reusable scraps?  
4. *Then* list the true gaps.

**Script:**  
> Before we name what we don’t have, let’s name what we do, including who in this room has solved a cousin of this problem somewhere else.

**Do this week:** Open one planning meeting with inventory, not the wish list.

**Anti-pattern:** Inventory as performative optimism. Be specific. Names, tools, artifacts.

---

## Practice 3 — Two odd tools, one problem

**When:** The team is stuck in a single-domain frame (only engineering, only sales, only finance).

**How:** Force a recombination.
1. Pick the stuck problem.  
2. Name two tools or disciplines that “don’t belong” together.  
3. Ask what a solution would look like if both had to show up.

**Examples:** Customer support transcripts + product roadmap. Finance model + frontline story. Design critique + sales objection log.

**Do this week:** One forced pairing in a real decision.

**Anti-pattern:** Random mashups with no decision path. End with: *what will we try in 48 hours?*

---

## Practice 4 — Rough artifact in 24 hours

**When:** The group is polishing a deck before testing anything.

**How:** Define the smallest thing that would teach you something true.
- A one-page prototype  
- Five customer calls with the same three questions  
- A spreadsheet that models one ugly scenario  
- A pilot with one team for one week  

**Script:**  
> We don’t need the finished version. We need a rough artifact by tomorrow that can be wrong in public.

**Do this week:** Ship one rough artifact before the next “we need more research” loop.

**Anti-pattern:** Rough as excuse for low standards. Rough means **early and testable**, not careless.

---

## Practice 5 — Debrief: what did you forage that you almost ignored?

**When:** After a win, a miss, or a scramble.

**How:** 10-minute close.
1. What did we use that was already here?  
2. What memory or person changed the approach?  
3. What did we almost dismiss as “not professional enough”?  
4. What will we inventory first next time?

**Do this week:** Add the debrief to one project retro.

**Anti-pattern:** Only celebrating heroics. Celebrate **foraging**, not overwork.

## Anti-patterns (name them so you can leave them)

| Anti-pattern | What it sounds like | Better move |
|--------------|---------------------|-------------|
| **Ideal-conditions waiting** | “We’ll start when we have X.” | Memory Forage + rough artifact this week |
| **Performative scrappiness** | “We ship chaos and call it culture.” | Constraints as design, not as identity theater |
| **Specialist silo** | “That’s not how we do it in our function.” | Two odd tools, one problem |
| **Refusing real asks** | “Bricolage means never resource the team.” | Forage first; then make the specific ask with evidence |

Bricolage is not a license to under-invest forever. It is a method for **starting** when the map is incomplete, and for learning what is truly missing.
